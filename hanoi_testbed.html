Subtractive vs Additive

The Tower of Hanoi, used as a scalpel to separate where a system pays — in navigation, or in scoring

Two ways to get from a scrambled tower to a solved one. You can navigate the known basin — prune the illegal moves and descend a small stack of subgoals. Or you can score the soup — assign value across states and pairs and let the weights decide. The first costs almost nothing to think; the second costs the whole pool. Below, both are alive: change the dials and the numbers are measured, not quoted. Softmax is the additive one, and it's the first to go.

IV. The peg-relabel test — does it know, or did it memorize?

Meta's sharpest suggestion, made runnable. A subtractive solver carries a rule — the third peg is always 3 − a − b — so it solves any board, including ones with the peg labels shuffled. An additive solver memorizes a table of states it has seen. Shuffle the labels and its remembered states fall outside coverage. To generalize, it must memorize the whole pool — exponential. The rule needs none of it.

V. What you're looking at, in plain words

The basin. With n disks on 3 pegs there are exactly 3ⁿ legal arrangements — every disk sits on some peg, and on each peg the disks can only stack one way, biggest at the bottom. That set of arrangements forms a graph that is self-similar: zoom into any corner and you find a smaller copy of the whole thing (a Sierpiński triangle). Self-similar means you never have to look at the whole map. You recurse.

Navigating it. Keep one number per disk: which peg it's on. To move a disk to its destination, the only spare peg is the one that's neither its current peg nor its target — and since the pegs are labelled 0, 1, 2, that spare is just 3 − here − there. No search, no scoring. You subtract the two options you can't use and the third falls out.

function settle(k, dest) {
  if (k === 0) return
  if (pos[k] === dest) { settle(k - 1, dest); return }
  const aux = 3 - pos[k] - dest   // the third peg, by exclusion
  settle(k - 1, aux)              // clear the smaller disks aside
  move(k, dest)                   // place disk k
  settle(k - 1, dest)             // rebuild on top
}

The stack is the cost. This routine visits an exponential number of moves, but it never holds more than n levels of subgoal at once. The live state is one destination and one spare per level — O(n). That linear stack is the real thinking. Everything exponential here is walking, not deciding.

The additive alternative. A scoring method instead assigns value across states (3ⁿ of them) or across pairs of states (9ⁿ of them) and aggregates. It generalizes only once it has seen enough of the pool — which is why, in panel IV, its accuracy tracks its coverage. Softmax attention is exactly this shape: every query weighs every key, O(N²), and whatever loses the weighting becomes the suppressed remainder.

VI. Why a trainer should care

grokking

The jump from memorizing the 2ⁿ−1 moves to holding the depth-n rule should show up as a delayed accuracy jump on unseen starts — not seen ones.

superposition / SAE

If a model learned the rule, a sparse autoencoder should isolate the peg-sum invariant and the subtraction. A hypothesis to test, not a result yet.

scratchpad tokens

Adding tokens to spell out the spare peg lowers loss only if the model is still scoring. If the subtractive stack has formed, they buy nothing.

induction heads

Copying prior move-pairs reproduces paths it has seen but won't yield the spare-peg rule. Measure off-path, relabeled starts to tell them apart.

credit assignment

A subtractive stack predicts stable credit across the n levels; an additive scorer predicts attention diffused across many past states.

attention

Look for heads attending narrowly to pos[k] and dest — not to the full history. Narrow is the tell of a rule.

VII. Held distinctions — what this does and doesn't prove

reconstruction ≠ record. The route the solver finds is a path back to the target. It is not the history the scrambler took to make the mess, and counting every step the recursion visits measures that reconstructed path, not the decision.

coherence ≠ truth. This testbed shows subtractive navigation wins on cost over a known pool, and shows why a memorizer fails under relabeling. It does not yet prove the routed representation is as expressive as the dense one on open-ended tasks. The load-bearing word is known: take it away and the n log N bound goes with it.

uncertainty ≠ dismissal. An open remainder — a board not yet settled — is a signal to navigate, not a defect to average away.
